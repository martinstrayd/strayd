The Archive of Excess: Backstage with Martin Strayd

By Rolling Stone Staff | Barb Dwyer • HOLLYWOOD, CA

Martin Strayd does not look like a man who spent the 90's keeping the wheels oiled on the rock-and-roll machine, but his eyes say otherwise. Sitting in a dimly lit booth off Sunset Boulevard, the independent Australian singer, songwriter, and self-proclaimed "recluse" handles a coffee with the calm precision of a man who used to tune vintage Gibsons for the world’s most volatile guitarists.

Long before his satirical, genre-bending singles started clocking up organic millions across TikTok and streaming platforms, Strayd—operating under his government name, Martin Straede—was the ultimate fly on the wall in the final, unedited era of Hollywood decadence. As a celebrity guitar technician, studio engineer, and fixer, he tuned the instruments and witnessed the madness of punk royalty like Steve Jones of The Sex Pistols, power-pop icons like Doug Fieger of The Knack, and various members of Guns N’ Roses and Duran Duran.

Today, he is completely independent, owning 100% of his massive, cinematic catalog—a commercial powerhouse he calls "The Vault." Rolling Stone sat down with Strayd to talk about surviving Kings Road, what Steve Jones taught him about the music business, and why he’s turning his backstage secrets into an auditory empire.

Rolling Stone: Your music deals heavily with luxury, nightlife, and extreme excess. How much of that is performance, and how much comes from your years working behind the scenes on Kings Road?
Martin Strayd: (Laughs) None of it is a performance; it’s an archive. When you live on Kings Road in West Hollywood during the peak eras of rock, you don't need to invent stories. You just look out the window or look at the guy passed out on your studio couch. I spent decades executing the chaotic "odd jobs" and extreme, late-night requests demanded by the rock elite. I saw the absolute highest heights of celebrity luxury and the deep, dark heartbreaks that happen when the house lights go down. My music is just the living soundtrack to that survival guide.
RS: You’ve worked with some famously intense personalities. What was it actually like being the personal guitar tech for someone like Steve Jones of The Sex Pistols?
Martin Strayd: Intense is one word for it. Working with Steve, or Doug Fieger from The Knack, you aren’t just tuning a piece of wood with strings; you are managing an aura. If a guitar goes down mid-set with punk royalty, you don't just fix it—you handle the energy in the room. It taught me everything about the raw, visceral power of music. But more importantly, it taught me how the industry works from the inside out. I watched how labels chewed up these massive icons, and it made me realize I never wanted anyone else holding the keys to my art.
RS: Is that what led to your "Anti-Pop" philosophy and the creation of "The Vault"?
Martin Strayd: Exactly. I watched the old major label system bleed artists dry. When I transitioned from being an engineer and a tech to putting out my own records like The Anti Pop Apocalypse, I decided to go completely rogue. No label, no traditional management. Because of my background as an audio engineer in those historic LA studios, I know how to produce, master, and format my own stems perfectly.
"The Vault" is my rebellion. It’s an independent asset layer containing all my masters, publishing rights, and metadata. While the old guard is still figuring out how to adapt, I've optimized my entire catalog directly for modern sync licensing—film, TV, gaming, and even AI training datasets.
RS: Tracks like "Devils Goin Public (Beelzebub)" have a distinct, theatrical bite to them. Are those specific characters from your Hollywood days?
Martin Strayd: Every song has a real face attached to it. "Devils Goin Public" is directly tied to that feeling of watching people sell pieces of their soul for a shot at the Hollywood sign. I blend radio rock, acoustic blues, and electronic elements to make it feel cinematic because those years were a movie. It was the last truly private era of Hollywood before everyone had a smartphone camera in their pocket. What happened backstage stayed backstage, until now. Now, it’s all going into the music.
